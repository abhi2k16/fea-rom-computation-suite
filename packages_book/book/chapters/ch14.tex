\section{What ``verified'' means in this book}\label{sec:vf-levels}

A claim that a piece of code is correct can be supported at six levels, and each level answers a different question. \Cref{fig:vf-pyramid} lists them. A unit test (level 1) asks whether the code does what its author meant on one input. A closed-form benchmark (level 2) asks whether that is also what the mathematics says, on a case where the exact answer is known and is not computed by the code under test. Convergence orders and the agreement of independent solvers (level 3) ask whether the error falls at the rate the method promises, and whether two different routes to the same quantity give the same number. For reduced models (level 4) the question changes to accuracy on parameters or loads that were not used in training, and to whether a bound held. A reproduction (level 5) asks whether a method described in somebody else's words, rebuilt from the packages without their code, recovers what its authors report. An integrated run (level 6) asks whether the pieces still work when they are connected: a model built by the mesh tools, solved by the engine, reduced, and used on data that none of the earlier steps saw.

\tikzfigure[0.98\linewidth]{ch14_pyramid}{The six levels of evidence used in this book. Lower levels contain many small cases and are cheap to repeat; higher levels contain few cases and test more of the stack at once. A higher level does not replace a lower one: it can pass while a component below it is wrong in a region it does not visit.}{fig:vf-pyramid}

Two rules apply to every level. The first is that the reference must not come from the code under test (Appendix~\ref{ch:conv}); a closed form, a quadrature of an integral, a much finer model computed another way, or a published number are references, the same solver at another setting is not. The second is that a pass is a statement about the cases that were run. The sections below therefore state, for each group of results, the case, the reference, the number, and what was not tried.

Three entries deserve a second look because they show the limits of the table itself. The balanced-truncation bound is reported as held in \cbtBoundHolds{} of \cbtBoundCases{} cases, not in all of them: the one miss is a case at rounding level, where both numbers are far below anything that matters, and the book reports it as a miss. The certified bound of Chapter~\ref{ch:freq} is valid wherever it is finite, and it is finite at few frequencies, so its guarantee covers a small part of the band. And the SVD dual basis of Chapter~\ref{ch:hyper} satisfies the reduced KKT conditions to rounding error and still gives negative lifted multipliers, which is why that chapter judges a reduced contact model by the lifted multipliers and not by the reduced residual.

\subsection{What the component checks do not show}\label{sec:vf-notshow}

The checks of \Cref{tab:vf-map} cover the element types and the analyses named, on simple geometries: beams, a plate, small plane bodies, one cantilever with two material regions. They do not cover three-dimensional plasticity against an independent reference, shells in contact, or the Koiter--Newton solvers; those parts are exercised by the unit tests of the repository and are not evaluated here. The reduced models are tested on one or two structures each, with parameters and loads drawn from the ranges of the training. Error is reported against a full-order model computed with the same engine, so a defect common to both (a wrong element, a wrong load) would not be seen by a ROM check; the closed-form checks of levels 2 and 3 are what guard against that. Wall-clock times are for the 2-CPU machine named in each chapter and for a dense baseline unless a chapter says otherwise.

\section{Reproduction of published studies}\label{sec:vf-repro}

A closed-form benchmark tests a component. A stronger test is to rebuild a published study from the packages alone and compare it with what its authors report. This was done for nine studies in nonlinear structural reduction, contact reduction, modal identification and learning for structural dynamics. The finite element models were built with \fea{}, the reduced models with \rom{}, and the scripts of each study are thin drivers over the two packages. \Cref{tab:vf-repro} gives the outcomes; the subsections give the evidence in a form that can be checked against the reports.

Four points govern how to read this section. First, the numbers are those of the author's reproduction reports, from runs made during development; they were \emph{not} repeated with the tagged release (\booktag{}) and are not generated by the macro pipeline of the other chapters. The reproduction drivers are scripts in the author's study folders, not part of the repository, so this book quotes them as report numbers and states where a study was or was not regenerated here. Second, ``the publication'' always means the numbers printed in the published paper, ``report'' means the author's reproduction. Third, an outcome is \emph{reproduced} when the published qualitative and quantitative claim was recovered within the tolerance stated in the text, \emph{partly} when some claims were recovered and others not, and \emph{not reproduced} when the main claim was not recovered; the definitions are repeated per row. Fourth, the reproductions are by the same author as the software, which is a limitation of the evidence, and most settings that a publication does not state (damping, training sizes, geometry) were chosen by the author and are listed in the reports.

The figures of this section are redrawn by \texttt{scripts/ch14\_assets.py} from the saved outputs of the study folders, and the numbers in their captions are computed by that script from those outputs, then compared with the numbers of the written reports (all agree; the script prints the comparison). The script can also re-run a study (\texttt{--rerun}, with \texttt{--jobs} worker processes) in a scratch copy, which for the long studies takes minutes to hours and was done on the author's 12-core machine, not for this book. The fluid-structure study keeps only images and a summary, so it has no redrawn figure and is quoted from its report; the network training of the frequency-domain PINN and the neural operator needs PyTorch and a GPU.

\subsection{Multi-fidelity-surrogate nonlinear ROM}\label{sec:vf-mfs}
Both examples of \citet{he2023mfsnlrom} were rebuilt. The flat beam (40 corotational beam elements, 117 dofs, three symmetric modes) reproduces the published linear frequencies to within 0.15\,\% (79, 218, 427 and 706~Hz in the publication). Both surrogates match nonlinear FE over the $\pm2000$~Pa pressure sweep with $R^2=1.000$; the surrogate takes about $5\times10^{-5}$~s per case against $0.84$~s for FE. Free decay is reproduced with $R^2=0.9971$ and $0.9898$ for the two initial conditions (publication: $0.9837$ and $0.9836$), and the backbone of the ROMs by harmonic balance stays within $0.96\,\%$ of the FE frequency over a 22\,\% hardening range. The shell model of the wing, taken as the reference model of the second example, gives the same level of agreement.

Four findings qualify this. The polynomial baseline (ICE) was as accurate as the surrogate or better on these smooth, weakly nonlinear problems, so the surrogate's advantage here is speed and generality, not accuracy. The publication's failure of ICE on one decay case ($R^2=-0.63$) was not reproduced. The time-integration scheme was reconstructed from garbled equations in the PDF text and diverged on the flat beam, so a Newton solve of the same relation replaced it. And the solid wing model reaches $R^2\ge0.96$ but its FE reference fails at large amplitude (an element aspect ratio of about 78 to 1), so the shell model is the reference; the measured hammer data of the publication cannot be recovered from the PDF. An early failure of the study (sweep $R^2=0.54$, then $1.000$ after a correction) came from a units error in the author's own driver, not from the method.

\subsection{Hybrid modal ROM}\label{sec:vf-hyrom}
\citet{yang2019hybrid} train a modal ROM from static solves and test it on forced transients. For the hinged cylindrical panel (726 dofs, 5000~N crown load) the nonlinear response differs from the linear one by $70.6\,\%$ of the peak; the ROM tracks it to $2.8$--$6.5\,\%$ of the peak with two to six modes, and its backbone follows the full-order curve to within $0.09\,\%$ with six modes, through a softening and then a hardening range. The cantilever plate with a hole is reproduced only in part: the modes agree to $0.3$, $0.9$, $2.8$ and $2.9\,\%$ and the full-order transient hardens as published, but with a smaller gap to the linear response ($12.8\,\%$ against about $20$--$40\,\%$). The ROM error is $10.4\,\%$ of the peak, only a little below the $12.6\,\%$ of a linear ROM, and the free-vibration check fails: the ROM hardens $4.7$ to $4.9$ times too much. The static solves reproduce the full-order equilibrium to about five digits, so the discrepancy is dynamic. The explanation favoured in the report (in-plane inertia at a free edge, absent from a ROM trained on static solves) is consistent with the contrast with the hinged panel but has not been confirmed by a fix, and the report records it as an open defect. The main script of this case was also not re-run end to end with the corrected element.

\subsection{Implicit condensation and expansion}\label{sec:vf-ice}
The flat beam of \citet{hollkamp2008ice} is the most mixed result. The four symmetric bending frequencies agree to within $0.2\,\%$ (57.8, 312.3, 771.1 and 1434.1~Hz against 57.8, 312.1, 770.3 and 1431.2~Hz), and the identified nonlinear modal force has held-out $R^2$ of $0.9998$ for one mode and at least $0.9993$ for four. The dynamic claims are not recovered: under band-limited random load the one-mode ROM has an RMS error of $19.5\,\%$ at the low level and $114$ to $136\,\%$ at the high level; a four-mode polynomial ROM with extra transient states reaches $2.2\,\%$ and $135\,\%$; a neural surrogate with static $R^2=0.9993$ diverges. The expanded in-plane displacement of the publication is not recovered either (with one mode it is a constant times $p_1^2$ and cannot change sign). Two regressors of different type fail in the same way, which points at the training distribution (static samples with independent modal amplitudes do not cover what a random response visits) and not at the regression. Whether iterated enrichment of the training set closes the gap was not tested.

\subsection{Matrix-free modal identification}\label{sec:vf-fsi}
\citet{liu2026fsi} identify frequencies, damping and mode shapes of a fluid-loaded plate with a Loewner pencil. The ANSYS and boundary-element model of the publication was not available, so a truth model was built with \fea{} (a Mindlin plate with frequency-dependent fluid mass from the Rayleigh integral); in air its frequencies lie within $-0.3$ to $+7.2\,\%$ of the published table, in water within $-1.1$ to $-9.3\,\%$. Against \emph{this} model the identification recovers all nine modes in each of four excitation--measurement configurations, with frequency errors of at most $6.5\times10^{-6}\,\%$ in air and $0.19\,\%$ in water and damping errors within $4.5\,\%$ in water. Mode shapes have a modal assurance criterion of at least $0.999$ for seven of nine modes and at least $0.95$ for all, and $0.9999$ for all on a denser grid. Damping is much more fragile than frequency under noise: errors above $10\,\%$ occur below about 60~dB signal-to-noise ratio, where the default screening thresholds, tuned on clean data, accept few modes. The outcome is therefore the method against the author's own truth model, not against the publication's data (\Cref{ch:nonint}).

\subsection{Frequency-domain PINN for a moving load}\label{sec:vf-fdpinn}
\citet{liang2024fdpinn} replace the time-domain moving-load equation of a simply supported beam by one ordinary differential equation per frequency and train a physics-informed network on the smooth, peaked spectrum. The network training is outside the two packages (it uses PyTorch). The packages supply the reference fields (an \fea{} Newmark and a modal-superposition solution), a moving-load class, and the frequency-domain ROM for the band-truncation study. For the three cases of the publication and three of its appendix, the analytical, Newmark and modal-superposition solutions agree to about $10^{-7}$ in normalised mean-square error or better. The cost of keeping only the spectrum below 5~Hz is $3.8\times10^{-5}$, $5.6\times10^{-6}$ and $4.0\times10^{-6}$ for the three cases, which is about 80\,\%, 8\,\% and 6\,\% of the errors the publication reports for its network; the \rom{} frequency-domain ROM reproduces the sweep with ten modes to better than $0.1\,\%$. The inverse problem matches the publication at its $10^4$-iteration budget (accuracy $100.0$, $98.9$ and $94.6\,\%$ against $99.6$, $95.8$ and $94.4\,\%$), but the forward problem is not reproduced: for the third case the field error is $2.4\times10^{-3}$ against $7.0\times10^{-5}$, a factor of 34. The runs used one seed, 5\,000 collocation points, single precision and at most 16\,000 iterations against 20\,000 points and up to $4\times10^{5}$ iterations; most of the inverse field error is the $EI$ bias (an exact solve at the biased $EI$ already gives 80--85\,\% of it), and the other explanations were not isolated.

\subsection{Hybrid hyper-reduction for contact}\label{sec:vf-hyb}
\citet{fauque2018hybrid} reduce contact problems with a reduced integration domain and a hybrid primal--dual basis (Chapter~\ref{ch:hyper}). The rope obstacle problem was rebuilt with the new module \anchor{rom_engine.contact_rid}. The best greedy run reaches a displacement error of $0.011\,\%$ (mean) and $0.084\,\%$ (maximum) against the publication's $0.004\,\%$ and $0.077\,\%$; an earlier gap of about 100$\times$ was traced to the size of the reduced integration domain, not to the solver, the snapshots or the load sign. The speed-up is not reproduced: $0.6$--$1\times$ at 100 nodes against $8.5\times$, because a 72-unknown complementarity problem dominates the 1.9~ms full solve. Reconstructed contact forces have an error of $8$--$10\,\%$ against $1.3\,\%$, and the report documents what the publication does not state. Two numerical failure modes of the reduced system, not discussed in the publication, were found: a singular reduced matrix when every node of the domain is a potential contact, and an indefinite complementarity matrix.

The 3D pellet--cladding problem (about 7\,000 free dofs) is reproduced only after a change. With the publication's training set (two simulations) the training snapshots are almost rank deficient (four non-negligible singular values), the basis has too few modes and the displacement error is $13$--$19\,\%$ against the publication's 1\,\%. Adding two simulations with intermediate gaps gives a displacement error of $0.05\,\%$, a stress error of $0.21\,\%$ and a correct contact set at every step, with a speed-up of $3.7\times$ (publication $10\times$). On meshes refined by factors 1.5 and 2 the speed-up rises to $11\times$ and $50\times$, with stress errors of $1.2\,\%$ and $1.3\,\%$, inside the publication's limit of $5\,\%$. The case departs from the publication's training set and is a single geometric parameter with interpolation only.

\subsection{Assembly reduction with contact and geometric nonlinearity}\label{sec:vf-asm}
\citet{nikiema2026assembly} compare POD, ECSW, DEIM and UDEIM for assemblies closed under preload. The 2D benchmark was rebuilt from the text (324 elements, 800 free dofs) with a new total-Lagrangian plane-stress element; with the parameters \emph{as stated} contact begins in the first load increment (at about 0.46~N) and not in the second, because the upper part is far too soft for the load. A sensitivity study over 28 variations found that the onset is set by the stiffness of the upper part, and two single changes bring it into the published window; the study carries on with the wider-strip variant, whose mode counts ($3$, $10$, $8$ against $3$, $11$, $8$) are close to the publication's. This is circumstantial and does not show that the publication used this geometry. With plain Newton, DEIM and UDEIM fail at $\varepsilon=10^{-6}$ while POD and ECSW converge, which is the central qualitative result; they do not fail at $10^{-9}$, which the report could not explain. The error is $0.94\,\%$ at $\varepsilon=10^{-5}$ (publication $1.2$--$1.5\,\%$) and the online speed-ups are $1.4$, $11.3$, $14.8$ and $12.8$ for POD, DEIM, UDEIM and ECSW (publication $3.4$, $13.8$, $15.8$, $18.4$). Domain partitioning with the package's indicator reproduces the full model to $0.2$--$0.3\,\%$ at threshold 0.1 but keeps 147 to 243 of 324 elements nonlinear, so its speed-up is at most $2.8$.

The report then builds a generalised 3D pipeline (total-Lagrangian Hex8 and Hex20 elements, an assembly builder, a full-order driver for sequential preloads). Hex8 locks in thin panels (down to 28\,\% of the exact bending stiffness), Hex20 does not, and plain penalty contact made Newton cycle, so a regularised law was used, a deviation. In a scaled butt joint the nine preload cases of the publication end within $0.3\,\%$ of each other, because the connectors close and the contact absorbs the extra preload, so the benchmark was changed to spring connectors. There POD and ECSW converge in all 36 runs (errors $1.7$, $0.65$ and $0.019\,\%$ at $\varepsilon=10^{-5}$, $10^{-6}$, $10^{-9}$), while DEIM and UDEIM as published fail in 34 of 36: their force approximation is good on training states ($1$ to $2\,\%$) and wrong by $10$ to $150\,\%$ slightly off them. The ECSW speed-up at $10^{-5}$ is $8.8\times$ for 87 of 480 elements, and falls to $1.4\times$ at $10^{-9}$, with an offline set-up of 54~s. This part is a pipeline and not a reproduction, and it covers one parameter, preload, within the trained range.

\subsection{Projection-based reduction of contact}\label{sec:vf-bal}
\citet{balajewicz2016contact} reduce contact with an SVD basis for displacements, a non-negative basis for the contact forces and a greedy sampler driven by a solve-free indicator; it is the method of Chapter~\ref{ch:hyper}. The static obstacle problem was run at the publication's size (39\,601 dofs): after 20 greedy samples the maximum error is $5.0\times10^{-4}\,\%$, a factor of 124 below the median of random sampling; the three indicator weightings gave identical selection sequences, where the publication saw differences. At the publication's worst parameter the displacement error is $1.5\times10^{-5}\,\%$ but the contact-force error is $52\,\%$ with an active-set overlap of $0.69$, which the publication does not report: displacement accuracy hides a poorer multiplier. In the dynamic obstacle problem (1\,521 dofs against 40\,000) the error falls by a factor of 127 to 288 over ten iterations, and the central claim is reproduced: the SVD dual basis fails (errors of about 300\,\% and negative multipliers) while the non-negative basis gives $0.058$--$0.083\,\%$. For two plates (3\,174 dofs against 20\,000) the non-negative basis gives $1.36\,\%$ with 40 modes and the SVD basis $41\,\%$. Speed-ups are against a different, faster full-order solver: $1256\times$ (publication $865\times$) in statics, $14.7\times$ measured in dynamics ($302\times$ in the publication; about $490\times$ extrapolated to the publication's size, not measured) and $13.5\times$ for the plates ($2229\times$). The publication's error of 52.8\,\% with 25 snapshots was not matched (13.6\,\%).

\subsection{A neural operator informed by a ROM}\label{sec:vf-frino}
\citet{yang2025frino} constrain a Fourier neural operator by the residual of a three-mode POD ROM of a 4239-dof Hex20 cantilever. Single-frequency responses reproduce: $1.5\times10^{-2}$ mean error at 5--20~Hz against $1.8\times10^{-2}$, and $8.5\times10^{-2}$ at 95--110~Hz against $0.3$ (the publication's value equals the error of not learning the forced response). Generalisation to multi-frequency and random loads does not reproduce with a plausible training set: $0.443$ and $0.691$ with 40 tones against $0.17$ and $0.098$; with 200 waveforms the best case is $0.128$ and $0.447$. The trained network is not the linear operator the physics requires (a superposition defect of $0.63$; relative error $0.0695$ at the trained amplitude and $0.976$ at ten times that amplitude). The physics term adds little: a data-only network is $3\,\%$ worse at low and $21\,\%$ worse at high frequency, inside seed noise. The publication's ``two orders of magnitude'' over a PINN holds only against a first-attempt PINN ($38\times$); tuned PINNs are $2.7$ to $11\times$ less accurate. For this linear problem the exact frequency-domain solution costs $18.1$~ms, the same order as the network's inference ($11$~ms), so its $139$~s of training is never repaid against it. The setup choices that the publication leaves open are listed in the report; the study uses one seed per configuration and a 3~GB GPU in single precision.

\subsection{What the reproductions show}\label{sec:vf-repro-summary}
Taken together, the studies show that \fea{} and \rom{} can rebuild nonlinear shell and beam models, train static-condensation, surrogate and contact ROMs on them, integrate those ROMs in time, compute backbones by harmonic balance, identify modal parameters from sparse data, and supply reference fields and frequency-domain ROMs for studies of learned models; and that in several cases the published quantitative claims are recovered within the authors' tolerance. They also show where the reproduced methods reach their limits: a ROM trained on static solves can fail dynamically when in-plane inertia matters or when the training states do not cover the response; a good displacement error can hide a poor contact force; and DEIM-type hyper-reduction can be accurate on training states and wrong a short distance away. They do not show that the packages are faster than other implementations: speed-ups are compared with published numbers only where the publications give them, and the full-order solvers and meshes differ. Where a study could not be reproduced the report says what the publication omits (mesh, damping, thresholds, definition of the error), and those omissions are part of the result.

\section{An integrated parametric pipeline}\label{sec:vf-pipeline}

The studies of the previous section test methods against other people's numbers. This section tests the book's own stack against itself, end to end, in the way a user would build a parametric model: a mesh from the generators, a system from \fea{}, snapshots, a basis, an affine reduced model from \rom{}, and then queries at parameters \emph{and} loads that no earlier step saw, reported by the convention of Appendix~\ref{ch:conv}. Everything in this section is generated by \texttt{scripts/ch14\_pipeline.py} in the sandbox named in \Cref{tab:vf-pipeline}, in one run each for the two meshes, and the timings are single runs (minimum over repeats of the loop in question), which vary by tens of percent between runs.

\paragraph{The model.}
A cantilever plate of $0.4\times0.2$~m and thickness $0.02$~m in plane stress is built as two blocks (left and right half) with Young's moduli $E_1$ and $E_2$ in $[0.5,2.5]\times10^{11}$~Pa as the two parameters. The stiffness is affine, $\bm K(\bm\mu)=E_1\bm K_1+E_2\bm K_2$, with $\bm K_q$ assembled once with a unit modulus in one block and zero in the other. The load is a traction on the free edge. Two meshes are used: a small one of \cplSElems{} Quad4 elements (\cplSFree{} free dofs) and a larger one of \cplLElems{} elements (\cplLFree{} free dofs); the larger one is the largest that fits the memory of the sandbox, because \anchor{rom_engine.affine.AffineDecomposition} takes its component matrices as dense arrays and a sparse matrix is rejected.

\tikzfigure[0.98\linewidth]{ch14_workflow}{The integrated pipeline. Blue: \fea{}; violet: \rom{}; grey: data; green: checks. The basis size is chosen on a validation set of the training kind; the two test sets are used once, at the end.}{fig:vf-workflow}

\begin{algbox}{Integrated parametric pipeline with held-out loads}
\begin{pseudo}
\Require mesh generator, parameter range, eight polynomial traction shapes $\bm s_1,\dots,\bm s_8$, number of training pairs $N$, tolerance tol
\State build the two-block system; assemble $\bm K_1,\bm K_2$ (unit moduli) and restrict them to the free dofs
\State draw $N$ parameters $\bm\mu_i$ by Latin hypercube and loads $\bm f_i=\sum_j c_{ij}\bm s_j$ with random $c_{ij}$
\State solve $\bm K(\bm\mu_i)\bm u_i=\bm f_i$ for all $i$;\ POD of $[\bm u_1\ \cdots\ \bm u_N]$ $\to\bm V$ \Comment{offline}
\State $\bm K_{r,q}\gets\bm V^T\bm K_q\bm V$ for $q=1,2$ \Comment{offline, once}
\For{$r$ in the grid of basis sizes}
  \State on the validation set (new $\bm\mu$, loads of the training kind): errors of the full state and of the four local quantities
\EndFor
\State $r^\ast\gets$ smallest $r$ whose largest validation error is at most tol
\For{each query $(\bm\mu,\bm f)$ of the test sets}
  \State $\bm q\gets(\sum_qE_q\bm K_{r,q})^{-1}\bm V_{r^\ast}^T\bm f$;\ $\bm u\gets\bm V_{r^\ast}\bm q$ \Comment{online, then lift}
  \State tip deflection, peak von Mises stress, total reaction and root moment from $\bm u$ \Comment{recovery: the same code as for the full model}
  \State residual indicator $\|\bm f-\bm K(\bm\mu)\bm u\|/\|\bm f\|$
\EndFor
\State report: errors against the full model on both test sets, offline, online, lift and recovery cost, break-even number of queries
\Ensure reduced model and its report
\end{pseudo}
\begin{algdet}
\item[Cost] offline: $N$ full-order solves, one SVD of an $n\times N$ matrix and two small projections; online per query: one $r\times r$ solve, one lift, and the recovery of the local quantities, which costs the same as for the full model.
\item[Fails when] the loads of the queries are not in the span that the training loads explored (the error stops falling with $r$, \Cref{fig:vf-pipeline}a); the parameter range is left; the local quantity of interest depends on the field in a way the basis does not carry (the checks below).
\item[Code] \anchor{fea_engine.solver.FESystem}, \anchor{rom_engine.pod.PodBasis}, \anchor{rom_engine.affine.AffineDecomposition}.
\item[Verified in] \Cref{tab:vf-pl-err}, \Cref{tab:vf-pipeline}, \Cref{fig:vf-pipeline}.
\end{algdet}
\end{algbox}

\paragraph{Training, validation, tests.}
\cplSNtrain{} training pairs are drawn, each with a different parameter and a different random mixture of the eight traction shapes ($x$- and $y$-tractions of degrees 0 to 3 in the coordinate along the edge). The basis size is chosen on a separate validation set of 20 queries of the same kind: the smallest $r$ for which the largest error of the full state and of the four local quantities is at most $10^{-3}$; both meshes give $r=\cplSR$ (the criterion is \cplSSelMet{} on the small mesh and \cplLSelMet{} on the large one). Two test sets of 40 queries each are then evaluated once. Set~A has new parameters and loads of the training kind (new random mixtures). Set~B has new parameters and a load shape outside the span of the training shapes, a vertical traction $a\sin\pi y$ with $a$ of random sign and size. The local quantities are the tip deflection, the peak von Mises stress, the total vertical reaction and the root moment about the out-of-plane axis; the last two are computed from the reaction forces $\bm K\bm u$ at the clamped dofs and are compared with the load scale and not with their own value, because a resultant can cancel for a load of mixed sign.

\paragraph{What the errors say.}
Every error of \Cref{tab:vf-pl-err} is below $10^{-3}$ on both meshes, and the held-out parameters and loads cause no larger error than the validation set did, so in this problem the selection on validation data transfers to the tests. Three observations qualify that. First, the full-state error of set~B does not keep falling with $r$: it stops near \cplBFloor{} beyond $r\approx16$ (\Cref{fig:vf-pipeline}a), while set~A falls to \cplAFloor{} at $r=24$. The sine load is not in the span of the polynomial traction shapes, so a part of it cannot be represented by any basis built from them, however large; a load outside the training span sets a floor on the error that the number of modes cannot lower. Second, the local errors are not ordered like the global one: for set~A the error of the total reaction is \cplSARy{} against \cplSAGlob{} for the full state on the small mesh, and on the large mesh the reaction and the peak stress are above the tip deflection by a factor of two to five. A local quantity that is a derivative of the field (a stress) or an integral of it (a reaction) depends on parts of the field that the global norm weights little, which is why Appendix~\ref{ch:conv} asks for one next to the other. Third, the cheap online indicator, the relative residual of the full equation, tracks the error across the two sets (Spearman correlation \cplSpearman), but within set~B, whose residual is nearly constant (\cplResidB) because it is dominated by the part of the load the basis cannot carry, the correlation is only \cplSpearmanB. The indicator is a screening tool, not a bound, and it says nothing about which part of a residual is representable.

\paragraph{Cost.}
The offline cost on the small mesh is \cplSOff~ms, of which \cplSOffSnap~ms are the $\cplSNtrain$ snapshot solves. Against a full-order baseline that re-assembles the stiffness for every query through \code{assemble_stiffness}, which takes \cplSTFomreasm~ms in the Python element loop on the small mesh, the reduced model pays for itself before the first query (break-even \cplSBeReasm{} queries). That baseline is the one a user who changes the material and calls the engine again would have, but it is a poor one: the stiffness is affine, so a full-order solver that forms $E_1\bm K_1+E_2\bm K_2$ and solves it sparsely takes \cplSTFomsparse~ms (\cplLTFomsparse~ms on the large mesh), against \cplSTRom~ms for the reduced solve. Against that baseline the break-even is \cplSBeSparse{} queries on the small mesh and \cplLBeSparse{} on the large one, and the online solve alone is faster by a factor of \cplSSpeedSparse{} and \cplLSpeedSparse. The workflow, however, does not end at the solve. Recovering the local quantities from the lifted field costs \cplSTRecover~ms on the small mesh and \cplLTRecover~ms on the large one, more than a hundred times the reduced solve, and the full-order workflow pays the same: the ratio of the two complete workflows, recovery included, is \cplSSpeedFlowSparse{} on the small mesh and \cplLSpeedFlowSparse{} on the large one against the sparse affine baseline (and \cplSSpeedFlowReasm{} and \cplLSpeedFlowReasm{} against the re-assembled one). A speed-up of the online solve alone, such as those quoted for reduced models in the literature and in the previous section, is therefore not a speed-up of the workflow whenever the answer of interest is a stress or a reaction. A reduced model that recovers stresses only at the few elements of interest, or a hyper-reduced recovery, would change this ratio; none of them is part of the pipeline above.

\subsection{The speed-up discrepancy}\label{sec:vf-speed}
Three different speed-ups of the two-region beam example have been quoted for this stack: $221.8$ in the README and the user guide (a 500-point sweep, $1653.23$~ms against $7.45$~ms, no machine stated), $361$ in the measurements supplied with the article (the same beam, $242$ dofs, $3.646$~s against $10.1$~ms, minimum over repeats of the whole sweep, on the author's 12-core machine, commit 33cde35), and $254$ in an earlier manuscript. A search of the repository and of the article finds $254$ nowhere, and this book does not quote it. The example script of the repository was run \cplRdRuns{} times in the sandbox of this book: the full-order sweep of $500$ queries (stiffness re-assembly and a dense solve) takes about \cplRdFomMs~ms and the reduced one about \cplRdRomMs~ms, and the ratio is between \cplRdMin{} and \cplRdMax{} (median \cplRdMed). The README value lies inside that range, and so would any figure within about fifteen per cent of it: the reduced sweep takes only about ten milliseconds, so a change of one millisecond moves the ratio by ten per cent. The measurements of the article differ for a second reason as well, because they take the minimum over repeats and not a single pass. The three numbers are therefore not in conflict and none is a property of the code: each is a ratio of two timings on one machine under one timing rule, with a dense full-order baseline (\Cref{ch:pod}), and the book quotes only the ratio it generated, together with its range.

\section{Limits of this chapter}\label{sec:vf-limits}

\begin{limitsbox}
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{One author, one machine.} The reproductions were written and run by the author of the packages. They are evidence that the packages can rebuild a method from its description; they are not independent confirmation, and the choices left open by a publication were filled by the same person who wrote the code.
\item \textbf{The reports were not re-run at the tag.} The figures are redrawn from saved outputs of the study folders, and the numbers in the captions are computed from them and compared with the written reports. The studies themselves (\texttt{--rerun}) were not repeated at \booktag{} for this book, and their drivers live outside the repository.
\item \textbf{Small models.} Most studies use meshes of a few hundred to a few tens of thousands of degrees of freedom, and reported speed-ups are against full-order solvers that differ from the publications'. No statement about large industrial models follows.
\item \textbf{Single runs.} Timings are single measurements and vary by tens of percent; neural-network studies use one seed per configuration. Differences inside that noise are not findings.
\item \textbf{The pipeline is a plate with two materials.} It tests one parameter pair, one basis size chosen on a validation set, and loads inside and outside the trained span (the latter sets an error floor of about $1.7\times10^{-4}$). It does not test geometry or topology changes, nonlinearity, or a hyper-reduced recovery, and the package's affine decomposition accepts dense components only.
\item \textbf{The residual indicator is not a bound.} In the pipeline it ranks queries but can be off in either direction, and local errors can exceed the global one.
\item \textbf{Open items.} The fluid-structure study has no redrawn figure, the network trainings need PyTorch and a GPU that this book's build did not have, and the modal frequencies quoted for the first reproduction (79/218/427/706~Hz in the publication) are not all retained in the saved data.
\end{itemize}
\end{limitsbox}

\section{Summary}\label{sec:vf-summary}

Evidence for a numerical claim comes at six levels, from a unit test to an integrated run, and each level answers a different question; a higher level does not replace a lower one. The component checks of Chapters~\ref{ch:elem} to~\ref{ch:hyper} establish that the parts do what the mathematics says on cases with a known answer. The reproductions show that nine published studies could be rebuilt from the packages, with their outcomes recorded as they came, including the failures: dynamic accuracy of statically trained ROMs, contact forces hidden behind good displacements, and DEIM-type hyper-reduction wrong a short distance from its training states. The integrated pipeline shows the whole path from mesh to held-out loads, reported by the convention of Appendix~\ref{ch:conv}, with offline, online, lift and recovery cost separated; stress recovery, not the reduced solve, dominates the workflow, so a speed-up of the solve alone is not a speed-up of the answer. The three quoted speed-ups of the example beam are single timings under different rules and none is a property of the code.

\section*{Exercises}\label{sec:vf-exercises}

\begin{enumerate}
\item \emph{(Derivation)} The break-even number of queries is the offline cost divided by the saving per query, lift included. Derive it, and show that if recovery costs the same in the full-order and the reduced workflow, the workflow speed-up is smaller than the speed-up of the solve alone and tends to $1$ as recovery dominates.
\item \emph{(Derivation)} A reduced model has a maximum error of $10^{-4}$ on training loads and a floor of $1.7\times10^{-4}$ for a load outside their span. Explain, with the decomposition of the load into a component in the span and one orthogonal to it, why increasing $r$ cannot lower the floor.
\item \emph{(Implementation)} Run \texttt{scripts/ch14\_pipeline.py} with a different validation set and a different candidate list of $r$. Report the chosen $r$ and the errors by the convention of Appendix~\ref{ch:conv}.
\item \emph{(Implementation)} Replace the dense affine components of the pipeline by sparse ones. Find where \code{AffineDecomposition} refuses them, and give the smallest change that would accept them.
\item \emph{(Implementation)} Add a recovery of stress at 20 elements only and measure the workflow speed-up again. State what changes in the break-even number.
\item \emph{(Interpretation)} The ICE ROM of Section~\ref{sec:vf-ice} fits the static modal force with $R^2=0.9998$ and still fails under random loading. Which level of Figure~\ref{fig:vf-pyramid} would have caught this, and what test would you add at the level below it?
\item \emph{(Interpretation)} A reproduction matches a published error to two digits. List three reasons this may not confirm the method, using the open inputs recorded in Section~\ref{sec:vf-asm}.
\item \emph{(Interpretation)} Read the speed-up discrepancy of Section~\ref{sec:vf-speed}. Specify a timing protocol (repeats, statistic, machine report) under which a single number could be quoted, and say what it would still not show.
\end{enumerate}
